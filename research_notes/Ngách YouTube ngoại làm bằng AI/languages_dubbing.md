## Q1. Which non-English markets pay high RPM, how competitive are they, which faceless formats work there, and who grew by localizing?

### Takeaway
Measured panels agree that viewer *country* (not language) sets CPM: Australia, the Nordics, Switzerland, US, Canada, UK lead; Germany/Netherlands/Austria are second-tier on advertiser CPM, and Japan/Korea sit around a third to half of US. But creator RPM for German/French viewers is much lower than their CPM in the one 2026 panel that publishes RPM (short views, few ads per view), so "German = near-US money" is not supported by 2026 measured RPM; the only non-English languages whose speakers sit mainly in top-RPM countries are the Nordic languages (Danish/Norwegian/Swedish), which YouTube auto-dubbing does not support. Competition is lower outside English is a vendor claim, not a measured fact.

### Inferences
- INFERRED: Dubbing an English video into German earns *German-viewer* rates, not US rates. On 2026 measured RPM (Dynamoi), a German/French/Spanish/Italian viewer is worth roughly $1.3–1.4 per 1,000 views vs US ~$5.1, i.e. ~25% of a US view; Japanese ~50%; Dutch ~60%; Danish/Norwegian ≥ US. AIR's June-2025 CPM (Germany $9.79 vs US $14.67 = 67%) is the optimistic bound. Dynamoi skews to music promotion (short views, few mid-rolls: Germany 2.8 min/view), so a 30–60 min history documentary would likely land between the two — but that is not measured.
- INFERRED: Because AIR's Education & Science niche RPM ($10.22) is ~4x the all-niche median ($2.30), the niche choice moves revenue more than the language choice. A German track on a history documentary is a *bonus* layer on top of the English niche decision, not a replacement for it.
- INFERRED: Highest RPM per speaker = Danish, Norwegian, Swedish, Finnish, Icelandic (small populations, top-3 RPM countries). None is in YouTube's auto-dub list, so a studio adding its own TTS tracks there would face no free YouTube-generated competition in those languages — but audiences are tiny (Norway ~5.5M people) and Nordic viewers commonly watch English content directly (not measured here).
- INFERRED: Kids content localizes well in views (all the big AIR cases are kids) but monetizes poorly (Kids & Teens RPM $0.33) and is Made-for-Kids restricted — not a revenue multiplier for this studio.
- INFERRED: Kurzgesagt's German channel (2.7M subs, ~10M views/month) shows a faceless animated science/history format can sustain a German-language audience at roughly 1/10 the English scale — consistent with German being a "bonus 10–20%" market, not a primary one.
- INFERRED: Arabic Gulf (UAE/Saudi) CPM is far below Western Europe in every panel (UAE $1.93 AIR CPM, $2.15 Dynamoi RPM); value comes from diaspora viewers in Germany/France/US (AIR claim), not from the Gulf itself.

### Gaps
- No measured RPM by *audio-track language* exists publicly (all panels are by viewer country). How much a German-dubbed view earns vs an English view from the same country is not measured.
- No measured competition index by language (e.g., supply of long-form history/sleep videos per searcher in DE vs EN). All "less competition" statements are vendor claims.
- US Spanish (US Hispanic) RPM: found no source that separates Spanish-audio views from the US by RPM.
- No source found on which faceless *sleep* or *long history documentary* channels exist in German/Japanese and how they perform; needs our own YouTube Data API pass (like the 2026-11-12 English pass).
- Swiss RPM by language region (DE/FR/IT) not found.

## Q2. How do YouTube multi-language audio (MLA) and auto-dubbing work in 2026, who has access, how are views/revenue attributed, and what are the AI-disclosure rules?

### Takeaway
Two separate features: (1) **auto-dubbing** — YouTube generates AI tracks for free, now on for every channel (Feb 2026), 20 target languages from English, Expressive Speech in 8 languages, experimental lip-sync for select channels, not editable, not for videos > 120 min; (2) **multi-language audio** — upload your own dub tracks to the same video, expanded to all creators with Advanced features (Sept 2025). Both keep all views on one video; YouTube reports >25% of watch time from non-primary languages for MLA uploaders (July 2025). Analytics break down views/watch time by audio track; viewer country sets the ad rate.

### Inferences
- INFERRED: For a faceless studio with *non-realistic* narration (a synthetic narrator voice that does not imitate a real person over illustrated/3D visuals), a TTS dub track is unlikely to require the "altered or synthetic" flag; a cloned voice of a real person would. CineFactory should still log the choice per episode (CLAUDE.md requires AI-disclosure checks in `cf_policy_check.py`).
- INFERRED (important for our niche): Sleep/history cuts **over 120 minutes are not eligible for auto-dubbing**, so YouTube will not give us free AI tracks on long sleep videos; own MLA tracks have no stated length cap beyond "roughly the same length as the video".
- INFERRED: Turning auto-dub on is close to zero cost (YouTube says no harm to original discovery), but it occupies the language slot — to upload our own better German track we must delete YouTube's German auto-dub first.
- INFERRED revenue arithmetic (ESTIMATED, illustrative only): an English history video with 100,000 views at a $10 RPM earns $1,000. If, as YouTube's average says, non-primary-language viewing becomes ~25% of total watch time, the extra ~33,000 views priced at German-viewer RPM (25–67% of US → ~$2.5–6.7 RPM at the same niche) add ~$80–220, i.e. **+8–22% revenue**, not +25–200%. The "3x" (Jamie Oliver) and "1.7x" (SV2) cases come from cooking/entertainment with mass Spanish/Hindi/Portuguese demand, where view gains are large but RPM per extra view is low.
- INFERRED: Velocity matters — BeMultilingual and AIR both report that tracks live at publish catch the recommendation push window; dubbing old catalogue mainly captures long tail.

## Q3. Tools: paid dubbing (ElevenLabs, Rask) vs open-source multilingual TTS (licences), LLM translation quality, subtitle localization

### Takeaway
Paid end-to-end dubbing costs roughly $1–3 per dubbed minute per language (ElevenLabs Dubbing v2 API $2.20/min; Rask $1.10–$3/min) — a 45-min video into 3 languages ≈ $150–400. For a script-first faceless studio, the cheaper path is to translate the locked script with an LLM, re-voice it with commercially licensed open TTS (Chatterbox MIT, CosyVoice Apache-2.0, Kokoro Apache-2.0) and re-time the edit. Avoid XTTS-v2, F5-TTS and Fish weights (non-commercial). Top LLMs reach human parity in many WMT25 pairs, but **not** English→Japanese.

## Q4. Risks: separate channels vs one channel with tracks; reused/inauthentic content; German/Japanese quality expectations

### Takeaway
Multi-language tracks on one channel are the low-risk default (one video, one view count, YouTube's recommended path); separate language channels win local search and suggested traffic once a language proves itself, but re-uploading the same video translated to many channels is exactly the "mass-produced" pattern YouTube's July 2025 inauthentic-content wording targets if done at scale with thin variation. Quality is the biggest commercial risk: AIR reports AI-only tracks losing 4–5x average view duration vs human dubs, and Japanese yukkuri TTS channels saw mass demonetization in Q1 2026.

### Cited Findings
- OBSERVED: Channel monetization policies (15 Jul 2025): "repetitious content" renamed "inauthentic content", clarifying it includes "repetitive or mass-produced" content; "no change to our reused content policy"; content must "Not be mass-produced, generic, repetitive or manipulative" — [YouTube Help, monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- OBSERVED: TeamYouTube (10 Jul 2025): "channels that use AI in their content remain eligible to monetize"; examples of mass-produced: "A channel that uploads narrated stories with only superficial differences between them", "A channel that uploads slideshows that all have the same narration"; creators must disclose realistic altered/synthetic content — [YouTube Community announcement](https://support.google.com/youtube/thread/356734251?hl=en&msgid=357121869)
- OBSERVED: Reused content = "not clearly your own original creation", may be "already on YouTube"; AI use is not automatically reused content — [YouTube Community FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/community-guide/271248162?hl=en&msgid=272674256)
- OBSERVED: MLA Help page frames one channel with tracks as simpler than "managing multiple language-specific channels" — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13338784?hl=en)
- OBSERVED (vendor): AI-only dubbed tracks gave a "4x–5x drop in average view duration" vs professional dubbing; Brave Wilderness Spanish: pro 5:19 vs AI 1:22; a kids channel Italian: pro 5–6 min vs AI 0:54; "low AVD from an AI track can drag down recommendation performance across the whole channel" — [AIR, good AVD on dubbed tracks](https://air.io/en/creators-spotlight/whats-a-good-avd-on-dubbed-youtube-tracks-the-data-backed-breakdown)

### Inferences
- INFERRED recommended path for CineFactory (owner decides): (1) keep one English channel per niche; (2) for each history episode, add own TTS tracks for 1–2 languages (YouTube's own advice), German first if test retention holds, Japanese only with a native reviewer; (3) leave YouTube auto-dub on for other languages on videos ≤ 120 min but review German/Japanese auto-dubs; (4) open a separate German channel only after a language track shows sustained watch time in Analytics (AIR's sequencing).
- INFERRED: Japanese is the riskiest target: subtitle-preferring audience (Morning Consult), WMT25 shows machines below human for EN→JA, and Japan's own TTS-narration genre is under active inauthentic-content enforcement — a TTS-dubbed Japanese track could be judged harshly. German is a dubbing culture, but it has a well-documented low tolerance for robotic voices.
- INFERRED: A faceless channel whose *every* video is a TTS narration over similar visuals already sits near the "narrated stories with superficial differences" example; translated copies on extra channels multiply that pattern. Keeping translations as tracks on the original video avoids duplicating uploads entirely.
- INFERRED: Because AI-track AVD collapse can reportedly drag the whole channel, each language track should pass a QC gate (native listener + watch-time check after 7 days via `analytics-loop`) before being kept — maker ≠ checker.
